ABSTRACT
Road damage monitoring requires more than identifying a defect in an image. Municipal authorities must know where the defect is located, how urgently it should be inspected, who is responsible for the work and whether the repair has been completed satisfactorily. This project proposes an integrated road-damage detection and municipal maintenance management system that connects visual analysis with an accountable operational workflow.
The system will accept road images and video accompanied by capture timestamps and GPS information. A road-damage-trained computer vision model will detect visible defects and assign supported categories. Location association, severity analysis, road health scoring and priority calculation will convert these observations into provisional incident records. A municipal alert service and dashboard will present the evidence to the appropriate staff for review and action.
The complete workflow covers road image/video input, AI damage detection, classification, GPS location, severity analysis, road health score, priority calculation, municipal alert, dashboard, officer verification, maintenance assignment, repair tracking, before/after verification, historical database and predictive maintenance. Each stage will preserve the information required by the next stage, while maintaining an auditable record of decisions and status changes.
The proposed technical solution combines a web interface, a Python application service, an independent inference worker, a geospatial database and media storage. Existing road-damage model resources will be evaluated for the prototype, avoiding the need to train a detector from scratch. Historical observations will initially support trend analysis; predictive forecasting will be introduced only when sufficient verified longitudinal data are available.
The system detects, analyzes, locates, prioritizes and alerts. Municipal officers verify findings, authorize assignments and approve closure, while maintenance teams physically complete repairs. The expected outcome is a demonstrable decision-support prototype with traceable incident handling. Detection accuracy, processing time, location quality and scoring agreement will be measured during evaluation; no completed deployment or achieved performance is claimed in this synopsis.
Keywords: road-damage detection; computer vision; GPS; municipal dashboard; repair tracking; predictive maintenance.

1. INTRODUCTION
Road infrastructure requires regular observation and timely maintenance to remain usable. Potholes and surface cracks can develop gradually, recur after repair or become difficult to identify in unsuitable lighting and weather. A systematic record of visible condition can help authorities organize inspections and document the response to reported damage.
Computer vision provides a means of screening road imagery for possible defects. However, a detected region is only an observation: it must be linked to a reliable location, interpreted in context and reviewed before maintenance work is approved. This project therefore combines image analysis with a municipal workflow, covering the period from first observation to verified repair and historical reporting.
2. PROBLEM STATEMENT
A maintenance process that relies on disconnected photographs, messages and periodic manual inspection may produce incomplete location details, duplicate complaints and inconsistent priority decisions. Without a shared incident record, it can be difficult to determine whether a report has been reviewed, assigned, repaired or closed with supporting evidence.
The problem addressed is the absence of an integrated mechanism that transforms road images or video into geolocated, prioritized and traceable maintenance cases. The system must support uncertain findings, preserve responsibility for municipal decisions and distinguish automated observations from verified defects. It must also retain comparable records for later condition analysis.
3. MOTIVATION
The project is motivated by the need to connect early defect identification with practical follow-up. A unified dashboard can reduce the effort of collecting evidence, locating cases and checking progress. Reusing an existing road-damage detector provides a feasible starting point for an academic prototype, while officer review helps prevent uncertain predictions from being treated as confirmed instructions.
The intended contribution is a complete maintenance information cycle. The application supports human inspection and coordination; it does not determine structural safety, authorize expenditure independently or perform physical repairs.

4. OBJECTIVES
The primary objective is to design and demonstrate a system that combines AI-assisted road-damage identification with a traceable municipal maintenance process. The specific objectives are:
•  Detect visible defects in uploaded road images or sampled video frames and classify them using the selected model’s supported labels.
•  Associate observations with capture time, GPS coordinates and road segments, while exposing missing or unreliable location data.
•  Estimate severity, summarize surveyed road condition and calculate explainable inspection priorities using configurable rules.
•  Provide municipal alerts and a dashboard for officer verification, maintenance assignment, progress updates and evidence-based closure.
•  Maintain historical observations and repair records for recurring-damage analysis and later predictive maintenance.
•  Evaluate detection quality, location reliability, scoring agreement, processing delay and completion of the operational workflow.
5. SCOPE OF THE PROJECT
The initial scope covers image uploads and batch video analysis, geospatial incident records, provisional scoring, dashboard views, configured notifications and repair tracking. The selected detector will target potholes and longitudinal, transverse and alligator cracks where its checkpoint supports those categories [1, 3]. Municipal roles will include officers, maintenance staff and an administrator responsible for configuration.
The prototype will support a defined road network or demonstration jurisdiction. Repeated observations of the same defect will be grouped without discarding their evidence. A case may be rejected, merged, reopened or returned for further work; these decisions will retain the responsible user, timestamp and reason.
Automatic structural certification, exact pothole-depth measurement, independent repair execution and guaranteed response times are outside the initial scope. Predictive maintenance will begin with historical trend summaries. Forecasting requires repeated, verified surveys and evaluation on later observations. Integration with actual municipal departments depends on their participation and configured access.

6. LITERATURE SURVEY
6.1 Smartphone-based road-damage detection
Maeda et al. describe road-damage detection using deep neural networks and smartphone imagery [2]. Their work provides annotated road observations and examines model operation on different computing platforms. It establishes a relevant basis for camera-based screening. For this project, the useful lesson is that acquisition conditions and processing resources must be considered alongside the model, rather than assuming that all road photographs are equally suitable.
6.2 Multi-national benchmark data
Arya et al. introduce RDD2022, a dataset containing road images from six countries, including India, with annotations for four road-damage categories [1]. It provides a benchmark for examining detection and classification. The proposed project will use suitable annotated samples as evaluation material where permitted. Local road imagery will still be required because a benchmark result does not establish performance on the intended deployment network.
6.3 Existing implementation resources
The RoadDamageDetection implementation by oracl4 applies a YOLOv8 road-damage model to images and video [3]. It offers a candidate reference for the inference component. Before reuse, the team will check checkpoint availability, class definitions, dependencies and applicable licence terms. The detector will be treated as one component of the proposed system; its output must still pass through location association, scoring and municipal review.
6.4 Cloud and data infrastructure
AWS documentation describes services suitable for hosting compute workloads, retaining media, managing PostgreSQL databases and distributing notifications [4–7]. These services can support the proposed architecture, but the application must implement incident rules, user permissions and repair state transitions. Infrastructure services do not supply a complete municipal maintenance process by themselves.
6.5 Project focus identified from the survey
The reviewed resources support visual detection and deployment choices. This synopsis focuses on connecting those capabilities to a complete operational record: a detected observation becomes a reviewed incident, an assigned task and a verified closure. This is the proposed integration goal, not a claim that existing research has never addressed maintenance management. Evaluation will assess the combined workflow as well as the detector.

7. PROPOSED SYSTEM
The application will maintain an incident record for each distinct defect. Uploads retain evidence, capture time and location source. A background worker performs frame selection and inference, storing the label, bounding box, confidence and model version. Compatible repeated observations will be linked to existing cases; uncertain matches require review.
An officer can correct findings, change priority, reject a report or approve action. Authorized staff assign accepted cases to maintenance teams, who record progress and completion evidence. An officer then reviews the before/after comparison and closes the case or requests further work.
8. METHODOLOGY
8.1 Acquisition, inference and location
Uploads will be checked for supported format, usable resolution and timestamps. Video will be sampled at a configurable interval, with sampling settings recorded. The selected pretrained detector will process usable frames. GPS captured with the image or synchronized to the video timestamp will be associated with detections. Missing coordinates require correction or a clearly labelled manual location; ordinary image pixels are not a source of GPS coordinates.
8.2 Severity, health score and priority
Severity will use visible damage category and apparent extent to propose low, medium or high concern. Detection confidence measures model certainty and will remain separate from severity. A provisional 0–100 health score will summarize unique defects within a defined surveyed road segment, with higher values representing better observed condition. The score will display survey date and coverage; unsurveyed segments will remain unknown.
Priority will combine configurable severity, road importance, report age and traffic exposure where reliable exposure data are supplied. Initial weights and thresholds will be reviewed against officer judgments and revised during validation. Missing exposure data will be labelled rather than invented. Neither score will be presented as an official pavement condition index or a guarantee of structural safety.
8.3 Historical analysis
Time-stamped surveys, verified findings and repair outcomes will support segment comparisons and preventive inspection suggestions. Forecasting requires sufficient comparable observations and validation on a later time period before operational use.

9. COMPLETE WORKFLOW
The sequence below preserves the defined process. Automated findings remain provisional until reviewed by authorized municipal staff.
Stage
Action and outcome
1. Road image/video
Capture or upload road evidence with timestamps and GPS metadata or a synchronized location log.
2. AI damage detection
Identify visible defects in usable images or sampled video frames and retain detection evidence.
3. Classification
Assign a supported damage category and flag uncertain observations for further review.
4. GPS location
Associate each detection with capture coordinates and a road segment; indicate location uncertainty.
5. Severity analysis
Estimate concern from visible defect type and apparent extent, independently of prediction confidence.
6. Road health score
Summarize deduplicated defects for a surveyed segment and display condition, coverage and survey date.
7. Priority calculation
Rank inspection needs using configurable severity, road importance, exposure and report-age factors.
8. Municipal alert
Send qualifying case details and evidence to configured municipal recipients; track notification status.
9. Dashboard
Show mapped incidents, evidence, scores and progress, with filters for jurisdiction and urgency.
10. Officer verification
Review or inspect the case, correct findings and accept, reject or merge the report.
11. Maintenance assignment
Authorized staff select a team, set a target date and record work instructions.
12. Repair tracking
Maintenance teams physically repair the road and record progress, notes and completion evidence.
13. Before/after verification
An officer compares evidence from the same location and approves closure or further work.
14. Historical database
Consolidate surveys, decisions and repair histories for reporting and recurring-defect analysis.
15. Predictive maintenance
Use verified historical trends to suggest preventive inspections; validate forecasts when sufficient data exist.
Operational records are saved at every stage. Historical consolidation in stage 14 supports long-term analysis; it does not delay incident storage.

10. MAJOR MODULES
Acquisition and validation manage uploads, image quality and location metadata. AI analysis produces detections, labels and supporting evidence. Geospatial analysis groups duplicate observations and calculates provisional severity, condition and priority. Municipal operations provide notifications, dashboard review, assignment and repair tracking. Historical analytics retain recurring-damage and condition trends. Administration manages roles, jurisdictions, scoring settings and audit access.
11. SUGGESTED TECHNICAL ARCHITECTURE
A React client will communicate with a Python FastAPI service over HTTPS. The service will validate requests, enforce roles and queue processing jobs. A separate OpenCV/PyTorch worker will perform model inference and scoring. PostgreSQL with PostGIS will store structured and spatial records, while object storage will retain original and annotated media.

Figure 1: Proposed application architecture
For an AWS deployment, EC2 can host the application and inference workers, S3 can retain media, RDS for PostgreSQL can manage case records, and SNS can distribute configured notifications [4–7]. The chosen instance must be benchmarked for the required workload. Failed jobs and alerts will be retried with duplicate controls; sending an alert will not count as officer acknowledgment.
Core records will include RoadSegment, Observation, Incident, Verification, WorkOrder, RepairEvidence and AuditEvent. Unique identifiers will link these records. Role checks will restrict approval and closure to authorized municipal staff. Database updates and media references will be recorded throughout the workflow; historical consolidation does not postpone operational storage.

12.2 Hardware requirements
A modern four-core development computer with 16 GB RAM and approximately 50 GB of free SSD space is a suggested starting configuration. A compatible GPU is optional for faster inference; its memory requirement depends on the selected checkpoint, input resolution and batch size. CPU execution can support small image batches, but the throughput of video processing must be measured.
Road capture requires a GPS-enabled smartphone or a camera accompanied by a synchronized GPS logger. A stable camera mount supports consistent imagery. Municipal staff need a browser-equipped computer or phone and network access. A dedicated embedded board or custom IoT device is not required for the initial prototype.
12.3 Data and operational requirements
Required inputs include road imagery, capture timestamps, location metadata, annotated evaluation samples and a defined road or jurisdiction map. The system also needs verified staff accounts, maintenance team records and configured notification recipients. Evaluation evidence must be kept separate from model selection data where practical. Media storage and compute capacity will be sized from measured upload volume and retention needs.
The prototype will use restricted access to incident media, encrypted network connections, backups and an audit trail. Unnecessary identifying details in road imagery should be removed or obscured before wider viewing. Cloud credentials will be held in server configuration rather than embedded in the client application.

13. EXPECTED RESULTS AND EVALUATION
The expected outcome is a working prototype that accepts road imagery, creates annotated detections, associates locations and presents provisional scores in a municipal dashboard. It will demonstrate the movement of a case from pending verification through assignment, repair tracking and officer-approved closure. Every demonstrated case will retain its evidence, status history and responsible users.
13.1 Evaluation plan
Detection testing will report per-class precision, recall and mean average precision on held-out labelled imagery. Evaluation subsets will include different lighting, surfaces and defect appearances where data are available. Adjacent video frames and repeated views of the same road sequence will remain in the same split to reduce leakage. Missed defects and false alarms will be reviewed as separate error categories.
Location assessment will compare reported coordinates with verified reference locations and inspect road-segment assignment errors. Processing tests will measure upload-to-result delay on the stated hardware. Scoring will be compared with independently recorded officer judgments; disagreements will guide rule revision. Duplicate grouping will be checked for both repeated cases and incorrectly merged nearby defects.
Workflow scenarios will include acceptance, rejection, reassignment, alert failure, repair completion, reopened work and unauthorized update attempts. Expected behaviour includes retained audit history, visible failure status and closure only after authorized verification. Results will be documented from actual tests; this synopsis does not assert achieved accuracy, successful municipal adoption or guaranteed repair speed.
14. ADVANTAGES
The proposed system connects detection to follow-up in a single record, helping staff determine what has been observed and what action remains pending. Shared evidence and duplicate controls can reduce repeated administrative handling. Configurable scores provide a consistent starting point for inspection planning while allowing officers to explain and record overrides.
Camera-based acquisition and reuse of an existing detector support a practical prototype. Before/after evidence improves the traceability of closure decisions. Retaining survey and repair history supports recurring-defect review and future planning. These benefits depend on the quality of observations, staff participation and validation of the complete workflow.

15. LIMITATIONS
Detection quality may decline under shadows, standing water, occlusion, motion blur or unfamiliar pavement appearance. Small defects can be missed, and road markings or repaired patches may be confused with damage. An ordinary monocular camera cannot reliably establish physical pothole depth or the structural condition of the pavement. Findings therefore remain inspection support rather than engineering certification.
GPS error, timestamp mismatch and incomplete road maps can associate a case with the wrong location. Repeated frames may inflate defect counts if duplicate grouping fails. Condition scores are only meaningful for defined segments with adequate coverage and comparable observations. Differences in camera viewpoint and survey conditions can affect apparent damage extent and before/after comparisons.
Network outages, compute limits and notification failures may delay results. Sending an alert does not ensure that it has been read or acted upon. Maintenance response depends on municipal staffing, materials, budgets and scheduling. Sparse or inconsistent historical records cannot support reliable forecasts. Data retention, access controls and ongoing model evaluation require operational attention.
16. FUTURE SCOPE
Future development can add offline capture with later synchronization, regional-language interfaces and integration with existing municipal work-order applications. Improved image segmentation may support more precise visible-area estimates. Additional sensing or calibrated capture methods could be evaluated for physical measurements, subject to independent validation.
A later predictive maintenance component can use repeated segment surveys, verified deterioration observations and repair outcomes to estimate risk over a defined future period. Forecasts should be evaluated on later data and compared with a simple trend-based baseline. When the history is insufficient, the application should display descriptive trends and inspection suggestions instead of unsupported numerical forecasts.
Further extensions may include recurring-defect summaries, workload planning and comparison of repair outcomes across time. Officer corrections can form a curated dataset for optional model adaptation. Any future automation should preserve the separation between AI recommendations, authorized municipal decisions and physical maintenance work.

17. CONCLUSION
The proposed project provides a complete information workflow for road-damage detection and municipal maintenance management. It begins with road imagery, develops a provisional geolocated assessment and supports inspection priority, notification, officer review, assignment, repair tracking and verified closure. Historical records create a foundation for understanding recurring problems and planning later preventive inspections.
The system detects, analyzes, locates, prioritizes and alerts. Municipal officers verify the evidence, authorize work and approve closure; maintenance teams physically complete the repairs. This division of responsibility is central to the design. The project will be evaluated through measured detection performance, location checks, scoring review and end-to-end workflow tests. Its value will depend on reliable evidence and accountable use rather than unverified claims of perfect accuracy or fully autonomous maintenance.
